There is a bacterium living on your skin right now that has no idea you exist. To it, you are not a person - you are a world. A landscape of temperatures and surfaces and chemical gradients. The bacterium was born, lives, and will die entirely within the geography of your forearm, having never conceived of the being that carries it. It is not stupid. It simply operates at the wrong scale to perceive you.

Now consider: what if we are that bacterium?

This is not a poetic metaphor. It is a serious philosophical question - and one that the scale of the observable universe makes increasingly difficult to dismiss.

<SectionBreak />

## The Anthropocentric Assumption

For most of human history, we placed ourselves at the centre of everything. The Earth was the centre of the cosmos. Humans were the purpose of creation. The universe - however one defined it - was understood as a backdrop built for us, a stage erected so that our particular drama could unfold.

Science has spent four centuries systematically dismantling this assumption. We are not at the centre of the solar system. The solar system is not at the centre of the galaxy. The galaxy is not at the centre of the universe. And increasingly, our universe itself may not be the entirety of what exists.

Yet the anthropocentric instinct persists - not in astronomy, but in the quieter assumption that the universe, whatever its size, is *for* something. That meaning flows toward us. That we are, if not the centre, then at least the point.

<Pullquote>
  The universe was not made for us. We emerged inside it - like foam on an ocean - and then had the audacity to assume the ocean exists because of the foam.
</Pullquote>

## A Question of Scale

Consider a simple chain of nested worlds:

Each entity in this chain is, to the one below it, essentially invisible - too large, too slow, operating on timescales and spatial scales too different to be perceived. The ant colony is a world. The city block it sits on is incomprehensible to the ant. The continent is beyond imagination. The planet - unthinkable.

We laugh at the ant's limitations. We should remember that something might be laughing at ours.

Call it *scalar relativity of significance* - the idea that what counts as "real," "alive," or "meaningful" depends entirely on the resolution at which you are observing. The label may be new but the intuition is ancient: every mystical and philosophical tradition that insisted the visible world was not the whole world was, in some sense, pointing at this. We have no principled reason to believe we are observing at the correct resolution. We have every reason to suspect we are not.

## What If We Are Wrong About Life?

Our definition of life is a checklist assembled from a single data point. We looked at the life we already recognized - bacteria, fungi, plants, animals - and extracted common features: metabolism, reproduction, response to stimuli, homeostasis. Then we declared these features to be the definition of life itself.

This is circular reasoning on a cosmic scale.

A star is born from collapsing gas, fuses elements in its core, generates energy, changes state over millions of years, and eventually dies - often violently, seeding the cosmos with the very atoms that will become new stars and, sometimes, living creatures. Is that not a lifecycle? Galaxies self-organise, collide, merge, evolve. The universe itself expanded from a near-singular state of extraordinary simplicity into the baroque complexity we observe today.

None of that *is* life by our definition. But our definition was built from a sample size of one pale blue dot, orbiting one unremarkable star, in one of two trillion galaxies. The humility required to call that definition universal has not been earned.

The numbers are not merely large. They are a rebuttal. The universe ran for 9.3 billion years before Earth existed. The idea that all of that was prologue - a warm-up act for our brief appearance - is not humility. It is its opposite.

## The Hierarchy Has No Ceiling

Here is the pattern that should give us pause: every time humans believed they had found the edge of everything, there was more.

Ancient peoples believed Earth was everything. Then we found a solar system. Then we found our solar system was one of hundreds of billions in a galaxy. Then we found our galaxy was one of hundreds of billions in a universe. Then cosmologists proposed that our universe is one bubble in an infinite foam of universes - each born from a different fluctuation in an eternally inflating substrate, each with potentially different physical laws.

The pattern is consistent and unbroken: *every final boundary has turned out to be a horizon, not a wall.*

There is no obvious reason this pattern should stop. Our "observable universe" is not the universe - it is simply the sphere within which light has had time to reach us in 13.8 billion years. Beyond it, space almost certainly continues. Perhaps forever. And beyond the multiverse, if such a thing exists? We do not know. We cannot yet know. But the honest answer is: probably something.

<Pullquote>
  Every civilization, at every scale, probably has a word for "everything." And every time, everything turned out to be just a neighbourhood.
</Pullquote>

## The Infinite Descent

The hierarchy does not only extend upward. It extends downward too, and with equal force - and the downward direction is, if anything, more philosophically vertiginous.

Humans are made of cells. Cells are made of molecules. Molecules of atoms. Atoms of protons, neutrons, and electrons. Protons of quarks. And below quarks? We do not know. Current physics cannot tell us whether quarks are fundamental or whether they contain structure we have not yet had the instruments to see. The history of particle physics is the history of things we thought were indivisible turning out to be made of smaller things. The atom. The nucleus. The proton. Each was, for a time, the bottom of the ladder. Each was not.

The downward infinite is more unsettling than the upward one for a specific reason: *we are built from it*. The vast scales above us are easy to feel small against. But the possibility that the matter constituting your thoughts - the neurons firing as you read this sentence - may themselves be universes of complexity at a scale you cannot perceive, that there may be something it is like to be an electron in the same way there is something it is like to be a person, is a thought that has no comfortable resting place.

We do not take that thought seriously because we cannot afford to. But the same logic that says the ant cannot perceive you applies here in reverse. The layers below us have no reason to be simple simply because we are unable to see them clearly.

The pattern of "zoom in and find more" has never failed us. The universe may be infinite in both directions simultaneously - no ceiling, no floor - and we are one layer in an endless stack, significant to the layers immediately adjacent to us, invisible to the layers far above or far below.

## The Tragic and Beautiful Arithmetic

There are roughly 8 billion humans on Earth. Of those, perhaps a few million are engaged in active scientific research. Of those, perhaps a few thousand are working on the deepest questions of cosmology and fundamental physics.

Against them: two trillion galaxies, each containing hundreds of billions of stars. An unknown but likely enormous number of planets. A universe 13.8 billion years old, still expanding, still evolving, still - by any reasonable inference - generating new complexity in regions we will never observe.

We are not under-resourced. We are *cosmically outmatched.*

Light from some regions of the universe will never reach us - those regions are receding faster than light itself can travel. There are parts of reality that are, by the laws of physics, permanently beyond our sight. We will never receive a signal from them. We will never know what they contain.

And we are mortal. Each individual scientist lives perhaps 80 years. The questions they work on have timescales of billions. The generation that identifies a new question will not live to see the answer. The generation that answers it will not remember asking.

## Other Words for "Everything"

If life exists elsewhere - and the statistical case for it is formidable - then somewhere in those two trillion galaxies, other minds are asking the same questions. They have their own word for the universe. Their own cosmology. Their own ancient debate about whether they are at the centre of things. Their own scientists, reaching the same humbling conclusion: that they are not.

They are as unreachable to us as we are to them. The distances involved are not merely large - they are prohibitive in a way that may be permanent. We will never meet. We will never compare notes.

This is its own kind of loneliness: to know, with near certainty, that you are not alone, and to know with equal certainty that the others will never be reachable. The universe may be full of intelligence and still be, from the vantage of any one intelligence, essentially empty. Full of voices that cannot carry across the void. Full of questions that will be asked and answered, independently, in isolation, an infinite number of times, by minds that will never know the others existed.

## What We Are Left With

The search will not end. Not because we will eventually find the answer, but because the universe appears to be structured such that every answer opens a larger question. We have been doing science for a few hundred years and the cosmos has grown *more* mysterious, not less.

An infinite universe deserves an infinite search. And perhaps that is not a tragedy - it is a description of the work. Each generation adds one small line to a story with no final chapter. We will not see the end. We barely got to read the opening.

But we got to be part of it. We emerged, briefly, from the universe's long complexity - and became the means by which it looked at itself and wondered. That wondering is not nothing. It may be, in fact, the most remarkable thing that has happened in 13.8 billion years.

Not because we matter more than the stars. But because, as far as we know, the stars do not know they are beautiful.

We do. For now.
